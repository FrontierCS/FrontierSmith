# Bessie's Scenic Road Collection

## Problem

Bessie now lives at intersection `1` and wants to visit her old friend at intersection `N`. She still dislikes arriving too quickly, but this time she has a more ambitious goal: she wants to **experience as much unique scenery as possible** along the way.

The countryside is an undirected road network. Each road has:

- a **travel length**
- a **scenic value**

Bessie will choose a **walk** from intersection `1` to intersection `N`. She may revisit intersections and roads, but the scenic value of a road is counted **only once**, the first time that road appears anywhere in her walk.

Your task is to output any feasible walk whose total travel length does not exceed the travel budget, while maximizing the total scenic value of the distinct roads used.

This is an optimization problem: better walks receive higher scores.

## Input

The input describes one test case.

- Line 1: `N R T`
  - `N` = number of intersections
  - `R` = number of roads
  - `T` = maximum allowed total travel length

- Lines `2..R+1`: `A B L C`
  - undirected road between intersections `A` and `B`
  - `L` = travel length of the road
  - `C` = scenic value of the road

### Guarantees

- The graph is connected.
- `1` and `N` are distinct.
- There is **at most one** road between any unordered pair of intersections.
- `T` is at least the length of some shortest path from `1` to `N`.

## Output

Output a walk from `1` to `N`.

- Line 1: an integer `K` — the number of intersections in your walk
- Line 2: `K` space-separated integers `v1 v2 ... vK`

### Feasibility requirements

Your output is feasible if and only if all of the following hold:

- `2 <= K <= 200000`
- `v1 = 1`
- `vK = N`
- For every `i` from `1` to `K-1`, there is a road between `vi` and `v(i+1)`
- The sum of the travel lengths of those roads is at most `T`

If the output is infeasible, the objective value for that test case is `0`.

## Objective

Let the walk use some multiset of roads. Its objective value is:

- the sum of scenic values of all **distinct undirected roads** that appear at least once in the walk

A road contributes its scenic value at most once, even if traversed multiple times or in both directions.

Your goal is to **maximize** this objective value.

## Scoring

For each test case:

- Let `O` be your objective value.
- Let `B` be the baseline objective value, defined below.
- Your test-case score is:

\[
\text{score} = \left\lfloor 10^6 \cdot \min\left(5,\ \frac{O}{B}\right) \right\rfloor
\]

If your output is infeasible, then `O = 0` and the score is `0`.

### Baseline

The baseline walk is the **lexicographically smallest shortest path** from `1` to `N`, considering the sequence of vertex IDs.

- First minimize total travel length.
- Among all shortest paths, choose the path whose vertex sequence is lexicographically smallest.

Its objective value is `B`, the sum of scenic values of the roads on that path.

Because all scenic values are positive and `1 != N`, we always have `B > 0`.

### Final score

If there are multiple official test cases, your final score is the average of the test-case scores, rounded down to the nearest integer.

## Constraints

- `2 <= N <= 5000`
- `1 <= R <= 100000`
- `1 <= L <= 5000`
- `1 <= C <= 5000`
- `1 <= T <= 10^9`

## Constraints
- Time limit: 4 seconds
- Memory limit: 512 MB

## Example

### Sample input
```text
5 6 11
1 2 2 5
2 5 3 5
1 3 2 4
3 4 2 7
4 5 2 4
2 3 1 6
```

### One valid sample output
```text
5
1 2 3 4 5
```

### Explanation

The walk is:

- `1 -> 2` (length 2, scenic 5)
- `2 -> 3` (length 1, scenic 6)
- `3 -> 4` (length 2, scenic 7)
- `4 -> 5` (length 2, scenic 4)

Total travel length = `2 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 7 <= 11`, so the walk is feasible.

Distinct roads used: `{(1,2), (2,3), (3,4), (4,5)}`

Objective value:

\[
O = 5 + 6 + 7 + 4 = 22
\]

The shortest path from `1` to `5` is `1 -> 2 -> 5` with total length `5`, so the baseline objective is:

\[
B = 5 + 5 = 10
\]

Thus the sample output would receive:

\[
\left\lfloor 10^6 \cdot \min(5, 22/10) \right\rfloor
=
2200000
\]

A simple shortest-path solution is easy to implement, but strong solutions will exploit extra budget to collect high-value roads through profitable detours.
